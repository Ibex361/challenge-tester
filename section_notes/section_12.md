## 1. What Is Market Analysis?
- Market analysis means **market study**.
- A good trader is **not a gambler**; he treats trading professionally. Before any buy or sell decision, he first studies the market, then decides.
## 2. The Three Types of Analysis
- **Technical** and **Fundamental** are the two major types.
- A third, **Sentiment** analysis, also exists (covered below).

## 3. Technical Analysis
- Focuses on **numbers**. An asset's price is a number.
- Example: an asset costs 10 birr today, 15 tomorrow, then 12, then 18. Technical analysis looks at this list of numbers, extracts a **pattern**, and tries to predict what comes next.
- In short: it reads the chart (the price history) and tries to determine tomorrow's price.
- Documentaries about trading often show that the people who succeed in the market are **math-strong** (e.g., math professors). Math is numbers, so people good with numbers tend to be strong in technical analysis.
- Technical analysis is all about analyzing the numbers to try to determine tomorrow's.

## 4. Fundamental Analysis
- Studies an asset's **true value** from different angles.
- **Demand**: if many people want an asset, its price is high; if few want it, the price falls. The first driver is the number of people wanting it.
- **Currencies**: a country's currency (e.g., the birr) rises or falls due to many factors:
  - Import versus export balance.
  - The country's policies.
  - Peace versus war.
- **Oil example**: the Iran–US war created a big gap in fuel supply, sending fuel prices skyrocketing. Fundamental analysis compares real oil flow, supply, and demand to find the **true price**.

### Birr / Kenyan Shilling Example
- About two years earlier, the official rate was roughly **55–57 birr per USD**
- Outside Ethiopia, no country accepted that rate. In **Kenya**, nobody would give Kenyan shillings for birr. Neighbors such as Uganda and others exchanged each other's currencies, but not Ethiopia's.
- Their argument was that the birr's true value was not 55 per USD. At that time **1 USD ≈ 125–126 Kenyan shillings**. They said that without government control the birr would be worth even less than the shilling, which is why they refused it.
- The government later let the birr float. Within a short time, **1 USD ≈ 160 birr** at the bank rate, while the Kenyan shilling now trades at about **130 per USD**. So the Kenyans were right: the birr was worth less than the shilling.
- Lesson: fundamental analysis looks at **war, a country's stability, its economy, and its policies** to judge the true value, and then asks what that value could be tomorrow.
- Fundamental analysis is **very broad and long-term**. Technical analysis, by contrast, also tries to predict **short-term** moves, estimating the price at a given time from patterns.

## 5. Sentiment Analysis
- Asks **what most traders are thinking**.
- If many people buy, the price rises; if many sell, it falls.
- Example (gold): if many traders are thinking of buying, the price is expected to rise; if many are thinking of selling, it is expected to fall.
- Some platforms show sentiment. For example, a broker may show whether most of its clients are selling or buying.
- **Limitation (stated explicitly)**: this is **not effective**. The market is not just that one broker, and the global market is huge. If that broker's sentiment is "sell" but most traders globally are buying, the broker's sentiment is useless. True market sentiment is hard to know because we can't know everyone's thinking.

## 6. Technical vs Fundamental: You Need Both
- Today's focus is technical and fundamental.
- There are **extremists**: some say only technical is needed, others say only fundamental. A real trader needs **both**.
- **Fundamental** analysis helps understand the **long-term** trend and idea of where a currency or asset is going.
- **Technical** analysis helps take **intraday** trades. Fundamentals tell us what is happening; technicals help us take today's and tomorrow's trades.
- Fundamental analysis alone makes it very hard to determine the price 5–10 minutes ahead.

## 7. Technical Analysts' Three Beliefs
### 7.1 Price Discounts Everything
- Technical analysts say **everything is included in the price**.
- Fundamental analysts study news and policies to predict price. Technical analysts say all of that is already reflected in price, so just looking at price is enough.
- **Presenter's view**: taking this to the extreme is not good, because there is no way to know in advance, for example, whether the US and Iran will fight tomorrow. Shocks like that, or a country's politics changing, cannot be predicted by technical analysis.
- Example: the **birr float**. Overnight, the birr went from around 50-something to 70-something in a single jump. There were rumors, but technical analysis would never predict it. That drop is explained fundamentally.
- However, **regular, scheduled market activities** (scheduled news, scheduled policy changes) are reflected in the price. We can see their pattern on the chart and recognize that "this price came during these economic changes".
- So both are used, and economic changes do show up in price.

### 7.2 Price Moves in Trends
- Price does not move randomly; it moves with a **trend**.
- Sometimes an asset has strong demand and the price keeps rising (uptrend). Sometimes the price keeps falling, possibly seasonally (downtrend). Sometimes it moves sideways and consolidates.

### 7.3 History Repeats Itself
- Not exactly, but **patterns** repeat. A pattern from yesterday comes back tomorrow, because "there is nothing new in the market".
- By studying past market history and patterns, we can make predictions, because these patterns are recurring.
- When we analyze, we look at past data and derive a concept from it.
- **Caveat**: this is a **scientific guess**, not certainty. Having seen a pattern in the past does not guarantee it will happen tomorrow.

## 8. Fundamental Analysis — What It Studies
### 8.1 Country-Level Factors
- The **interest rate** of a country's currency.
- The country's **economy**.
- Its **politics**: stability, government changes, and elections. All of these affect that country's market.

### 8.2 Supply and Demand
- For any asset, it studies how much supply and demand there is.
- If supply is high and demand falls, the price drops a lot. If supply is small and demand rises strongly, the price rises a lot.

#### Birr/USD Example
- When demand for the dollar rises a lot and dollars are scarce in the market, the dollar price rises.
- Because government control over the dollar was heavy, the true price was not visible at the bank, but the **black market** moved up and down with demand.
- When banks lack dollars, there is scarcity, everyone goes to the black market, demand is high, and the dollar supply is short, so the price jumps.
- When banks start supplying birr/dollars and have dollars available, the black-market price falls.
- The government gives dollars to banks through **auctions**. If banks hold dollars, the black-market price falls because supply increases. If dollars are available in many places, nobody goes to the black market, and the scarcity fades.

#### Covid Mask Example
- At the Covid peak, one mask sold for up to **400 birr**. Today it is about **3 for 10 birr**.
- Back then, demand was huge and supply small, because few pharmacies held masks. Demand surged while supply stayed small, so the price rose.
- As supply stabilized and demand fell, prices dropped.
- This is **supply and demand**, the fundamentals. Fundamental analysis studies how big supply is and how big demand is.

### 8.3 Policies and Events
- Fundamental analysis also studies policies and events, which it believes **shape the market**, especially the global market.
- **Trump**: after he took office in 2025, he issued many executive orders and created strong impulses in the market. The dollar's value fluctuated a lot, and one Trump statement was seen shaking the market.
- **Current global events**: the Iran–US war is strongly affecting the market. The Russia–Ukraine war also strongly affected it.
- Summary: fundamental analysis is an **economic concept**; it examines many economic concepts to judge where an asset's price is going.
- **Technical analysis**, by contrast, is "crunching numbers": look at the numbers, then the chart, extract a pattern, and predict what comes next.
