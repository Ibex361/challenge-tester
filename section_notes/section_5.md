# 5: Currency Pairs

## 1. Why Currencies Are Always Traded in Pairs
- No single asset is traded completely "alone" — value only exists in comparison to something else.
- Even when an instrument looks like a single name (e.g., "Gold" written alone, or "US Oil"/"UK Oil"), it is implicitly being compared to the US dollar behind the scenes.
- This is why trading instruments are structured as **pairs**.

## 2. Base Currency vs. Quote Currency
- In a pair, the **first-listed** currency = **base currency**.
- The **second-listed** currency = **quote currency**.
- Example given: **EUR/USD** → EUR is being compared against USD.
- Example given: **USD/JPY** → comparing USD against the Japanese Yen.
- Currency naming convention: usually [country code] + [currency initial]. Example given: **USD** = "US" + "D" (Dollar). Not a universal rule, but common.
- Example given: **ETB** = Ethiopian Birr.
- Worked numeric example: if **USD/ETB = 160**, this means: base currency (USD) = 1 unit, and quote currency (ETB) tells you 1 USD converts to 160 ETB.

## 3. Bid, Ask, and Spread
- Prices are always shown as **two numbers together**, never one.
- **Ask price** = higher number, typically shown on the right. This is the broker's *selling* price to you — i.e., what **you pay to buy**.
- **Bid price** = lower number, typically shown on the left. This is the broker's *buying* price from you — i.e., what **you receive when you sell**.
- Memory aid given: "Ask" ~ you're asking "how much do I need to give you to buy from you" → Ask = your buy price.
- Bid is always lower than Ask.
- **Spread** = difference between Ask and Bid. This is one of the broker's main sources of profit — brokers buy low (bid) and sell high (ask).
- Same bid/ask logic applies at banks too, when exchanging currency in person.

## 4. Pip and Point
- **Pip** ("Percentage in Point"): traditionally the smallest standard unit of price movement.
  - For most currency pairs: the **4th decimal place**.
  - For JPY-quoted pairs: the **2nd decimal place**.
- As electronic trading advanced, brokers began showing an extra digit of precision:
  - This extra digit is called a **Point** (or **fractional pip**) — one-tenth of a pip.
  - For most pairs: the **5th decimal place**.
  - For JPY pairs: the **3rd decimal place**.
- Some brokers display this 5th-digit point as a superscript or in a different color.
- Worked example given in the video: comparing two quoted prices with a difference of "1.2" in that final digit position → described as either **2 pips**, or equivalently **12 points**.
- Another example given: a difference of "1.1" in that position → described as **11** (points/pips difference, same logic).
- Reminder: pip/point terminology is for trader communication and convenience (e.g., saying "the market moved X pips" or "I made X pips of profit") — it's not deeply technical, just a shared way of talking about movement.
- Important caveat stated explicitly: **pip and point conventions differ for non-currency instruments** — Gold, indices, stocks, and crypto each may use a different number of decimal places, so this 4th/5th decimal rule is specific to currency pairs. For Gold, for example, the digit right after the decimal point was described as functioning like the "pip" for that instrument (different from standard currency pairs).

## 5. The Three Categories of Currency Pairs
Stated explicitly: pairs are divided into three categories based on trading volume/demand in the market.

### A. Major Pairs
- Must include **USD** as either the base or the quote currency.
- Paired with one of the other most heavily traded world currencies.
- Because they are the most heavily traded pairs globally, they are highly **liquid** (lots of buyers and sellers, so orders fill quickly).
- Because of high liquidity, brokers can offer very **tight, stable spreads** — the video states the spread on major pairs is "not variable" (i.e., stays consistently narrow) because of the asset's major/liquid status.
  - Note: **no specific spread number/value for any single pair (including USD/JPY) is given anywhere in the transcript** — the discussion stays at the general characteristic level (tight & stable), not a specific figure.
- Stated ranking of global trade dominance: **USD is the most heavily traded currency**, followed by **EUR**, then **GBP**.
- Because retail traders (individuals) generally trade in small amounts, execution is fast for them; the video notes that even large institutions trading major pairs in large volume still benefit from high liquidity, meaning major pairs stay liquid "for them too."

### B. Minor Pairs (a.k.a. Cross Pairs)
- Definition: two **major** currencies paired with each other, **without USD** involved.
- Example given as the **first** example: **EUR/GBP**
- Example given as the **second** example mentioned by name in the video's list of majors-paired-without-USD: **GBP/JPY** (Great Britain Pound vs Japanese Yen) — mentioned right after EUR/GBP in the same breath as the video lists out major currencies being combined with each other (Great Britain Pound, Japanese Yen, Australian Dollar, New Zealand Dollar were all named as currencies that get cross-combined).
- Characteristics: moderate liquidity — spread is **wider than majors, narrower than exotics**.
- Because these pairs involve less liquidity than majors, spreads become wider/more variable.

### C. Exotic Pairs
- Definition: a **major currency paired with a currency from a smaller/developing economy**.
- Examples of currencies mentioned in this context: **US Dollar vs Mexican Peso**, **US Dollar vs Turkish Lira**.
- Characteristics:
  - Lower demand/volume in the market → traders "self-identify" these as niche/low-demand pairs.
  - Much **lower liquidity**, resulting in **very wide spreads**.
  - Order execution can be **slow** due to low liquidity.
  - Higher chance of **requotes** — the broker may notify you that the price you tried to trade at is no longer available and offer a new price instead (this can happen repeatedly, e.g. up to ~10 times per the video's example).
  - Overall: small volume, small liquidity, large spread.

## 6. Commodity Pairs (a special subgroup within Major Pairs)
- Defined as major pairs that have a special/notable link with a specific export commodity of that country — though the video is careful to note the commodity is never the *only* factor moving the currency (interest rates, broader economic data, etc. also matter).
- Three specific commodity pairs identified, in this order:
  1. **USD/CAD** — Canada is a major oil exporter. When oil prices rise, CAD tends to strengthen (price goes up); when oil prices fall, CAD tends to weaken.
  2. **AUD/USD** — Australia has a special connection to gold prices (described as a major gold exporter).
  3. **NZD/USD** — New Zealand's dollar has a special link to dairy products/agricultural exports (milk, cheese, etc.).
