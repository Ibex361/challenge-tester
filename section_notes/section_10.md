# Section 10: How Prop Firms Work
*(Full content extracted from video & module)*

## 1. Definition: What Is a Prop Firm?
- **Core working principle**: prop firms design various **challenges** in order to find good traders. Traders who pass these challenges are given **funding** by the firm. The trader then trades with that money, and any profit made is **split/shared between the trader and the institution/firm**.
- So, they don't just fund you outright — first you must **prove you're a good trader**, and this proving stage is called the **Evaluation Phase**.

## 2. The Evaluation Phase
- During the Evaluation Phase, you're given various **rules** to trade under. If you follow those rules and reach a specified **target**, you pass and become funded (i.e., the firm gives you the money).
- Evaluation phase structures differ between institutions — even a single institution may offer multiple evaluation phase types:
  - Some skip evaluation entirely and offer a **direct/instant funding** option.
  - Some offer a **1-step** evaluation phase. Some might even offer a **3-step** evaluation.
  - But the **most common** structure is a **2-step evaluation phase** — pass certain targets across two stages, then become funded.
- **Evaluation is not free** — you pay to enter and participate in the challenge. The specific cost varies by prop firm, but **it's tied to the size of the account you want to purchase.**
- Example account sizes mentioned that can be purchased: **starting around $2,500** as a common minimum, going up to **$5,000**, $10k, $50k, **$100,000**, etc. — the larger the account you want to be funded with, the more it costs to purchase.
## 3. Common Rules During Evaluation
Rules vary between prop firms, but the following are common/standard across the industry:

### Daily Drawdown
- A limit on how much you can lose **in a single day**. Framed as: "you cannot lose more than X in one day" — designed to force proper risk management.
- **Most common daily drawdown limit: 5%.**
- Example given on daily drawdown rule: for a $10,000 account, 5% = $500 → you cannot lose more than $500 in a single day. If you lose more than $500 within one day, the evaluation is failed — the challenge money is not refunded, and you'd need to purchase another challenge to try again.

### Total Drawdown
- A separate, overall loss limit for the account (not per-day).
- **Most common: 10%**, though this varies depending on the account/plan purchased — 10% is common in 2-step evaluations, but it may be slightly lower than 10% for 1-step evaluations.
- Example given on total drawdown rule: for a $10,000 account with a 10% total drawdown, you cannot let the account fall below $9,000 total — going below that fails the challenge.

### Consistency Rule (a newer, more recently introduced rule)
- Introduced by many firms to address traders who were passing challenges with a single profitable trade.
- This rule requires that you remain **profitable across multiple separate days** — not all in one trade/day. This rule is not universal across all firms, but is common.
- Common implementation: an account must be profitable on **at least 3 separate days**. 
- Most firms define "profitable day" as growing the account balance by a minimum amount (commonly by around **0.5%**).
- Some prop firms additionally require trading on **at least a minimum number of days** to pass a given phase.
- Rule specifics vary by prop firm, but these (Daily Drawdown, Total Drawdown, Consistency Rule) represent the **common industry-standard rules**. Individual firms may add their own additional rules on top of these.

## 4. What Happens Once You Pass?
- Once you pass the evaluation phase(s), you are given an account with the money — e.g., if you purchased/participated with a $10,000-size challenge, you are given a $10,000 account to trade with.
- The same rules from the evaluation generally **still apply** after becoming funded — you must continue trading within those rules; breaking them will result in your funded account being taken away.
- Once profitable without breaching the rules, you can **request a payout** of that profit.
- **Payout frequency** varies by prop firm — e.g., some allow only **twice per month**.
- **Profit split** when requesting payout also varies by firm — again the **common industry standard is 80/20**: you take **80%**, the firm takes **20%**. Some firms offer **90/10** (you take 90%, they take 10%).

## 5. Important Clarification: What "Funded" Actually Means?
- Common misconception: people assume that once you pass the challenge and become "funded," you're given an account with **real money**, and profits you make are shared with the firm from that real trading.
- **This is not accurate.** The account you're actually given after passing, in **most prop firms**, is a **Demo account**.
- Because it's technically a Demo account, the "profit" you generate there is **not real profit**. What you're being paid out with later is **not literally your trading gains** — it's money the firm pays you **out of their own pocket, which eventually comes from the evaluation fee everyone pays**.
- Some (**rare**) prop firms do eventually convert genuinely consistent traders to **live/real accounts** after they've demonstrated strong ongoing consistency — but this is very as uncommon; the majority of firms keep the funded trader on a Demo account and pay them out of pocket.

## 6. The Actual Business Model of Prop Firms
- At the surface level, it might seem prop firms want profitable trader so that they would get more splits from the traders' profit. But this is not true because the traders are on Demo. If lots of good traders were funded and made lots of profit, this would cost the firm through payouts.
- **The actual business model**: revenue comes primarily from the **challenge/evaluation fees** people pay to enter. Remember, roughly **90%+** of new traders **fail** in trading. That challenge fee is treated as **profit** for the firm.
- When a trader becomes profitable in prop firms and requests payouts, it's from **that challenge-fee profit pool** that firms pay out to the trader— i.e., you become an **expense** paid for out of the money collected from everyone.
- Because of this business model, it is reasonable to suspect manipulators in the market. Some firms could have an incentive to prevent a consistently profitable trader from continuing to be profitable — since a profitable trader represents an ongoing expense for the firm.

## 7. Real-World Example: "My Forex Funds"
- **My Forex Funds**: a prop firm that was, around **2021**, very popular in Ethiopia.
- The company was **shut down by U.S. and Canadian government lawsuits** based on allegations.
- According to what came out in the allegations:
  1. They **lied about their business model** — publicly claiming that once you're funded, you receive a **real account**, and that your payouts came from genuine trading profits from that real account — but the accounts given were actually **Demo accounts**. After this incident, currently almost every firm openly tells you they are using demo account.
  2. They manipulated prices **against traders who became consistently profitable**.
- Remember that that My Forex Funds had a strong public reputation before this — with approximately **4.8-star on Trustpilot rating** — yet still turned out to have these issues, showing that such things can happen in the business.

## 8. The Overall Take On Using Prop Firms
- None of the above means "never use prop firms at all."
- **Where prop firms can be a good option**:
  1. For traders in contexts like Ethiopia, who may only have access to **small trading accounts**, prop firms can be a good way to try to **access larger capital**.
  2. They can be useful for **learning discipline** — e.g. trading strictly according to rules, and teaching yourself.
- **Recommendation**: be careful when choosing a firm. check that it is **regulated, licenced and with good reputation**. Verify it has no price manipulation, and pays out properly.
- Remember, this business model, when done properly, is a legitimate model that financial institutions/governments do trust and license appropriately. 
- Note this caution isn't unique to prop firms — there are also many bad-practice brokers. The recommendation there is similarly to pick a regulated, reputable broker.
- **However — regarding prop firms specifically for a long-term career**: prop firms are **not** recommended as something to build a long-term trading career around once you have capital, for the following reasons:
  - Successful traders are an expense to any prop firm. So, they don't want you stay with them. Those firms who will upgrade successful traders to live account are very rare.
  - Many prop firms have **many hidden rules**. Your account might get terminated due to a break of a rule you were not even shown. Even you the know the rules well, the firms might use that rule against you.
  - In summary: prop firms are good initially to get capital and discipline. But after some successful payouts, they want to get you out somehow, so it's going to be a fight and you will not have good experience, in the long term once you have enough capital.
- So **once you have accumulated your own capital**, it is **better to choose a reputable broker and trade on your own live account** rather than staying with prop firms long-term.
