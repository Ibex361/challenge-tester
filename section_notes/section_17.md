## 0. Intro
- **Why it matters:** interest rate is the **major thing** we look at in fundamentals. Other news and events are also understood in connection with interest. Understanding interest rates and their effect on a country's currency is core to doing fundamental analysis.

## 1. Interest Rate and Currency Value (US Example)
- Suppose the US Federal Reserve is **raising** interest rates aggressively. A rate increase is called **hawkish**.
- Investors who are in the US stay there, because interest rates in the US are high. Whether they invest in banks, buy **government bonds**, or buy various other investment assets in the US, the return is high because the US dollar rate is high.
- So the **investment potential of the US rises**, and demand for the **USD** keeps increasing.
- With USD demand rising, many global investors convert their money from various currencies into **US dollars**.
- If dollar demand rises, the dollar **appreciates**.
- **Rule:** when a country's central bank raises interest rates, its currency **appreciates** and its value rises.
- If rates are **cut**, the opposite happens: that country's investment potential falls, its currency's **attractiveness falls**, demand falls, and with no demand the currency's value falls.
- These are not direct, mechanical rules, because monetary policy is very complicated. So the next step is to explain in detail how interest relates to the economy.

## 2. Interest Rate and the Economy
- When a central bank decides to raise or cut rates, it looks at the **whole economy** the country is in. It does not simply say "I'll raise rates so my currency value rises".
- Raising rates has implications for the economy. Likewise, a bank can't simply say "I won't cut rates so my currency value doesn't fall", because that also has an effect on the economy.
- So central banks look at **many factors** when making rate decisions.

### 2.1 The Benchmark Rate
- A central bank (e.g., the US Fed, or the National Bank of Ethiopia) sets a **benchmark interest rate**.
- This is **not** the rate the bank gives us directly when we borrow or save. Commercial banks set their own rates based on their interaction with the central bank, built on top of the benchmark.
- The interest that commercial banks give us (on savings) and charge businesses (on loans) is **derived from this benchmark**.
- When the central bank's rate rises, these rates rise; when it falls, they fall.

### 2.2 When the Central Bank Raises Rates (a chain of effects)
1. **Cost of borrowing rises.** If I borrow from a bank, the interest I pay later rises, so my desire to borrow falls and fewer people borrow. Businesses are the same.
2. **Spending falls.**
   - People stop borrowing, and businesses stop borrowing to expand.
   - People also stop spending their own money, because saving in a bank now pays more interest. They start saving and even add to their bank deposits.
   - The cycle of spending money slows, and the economy becomes **slow**.
   - **Car example:** a car manufacturer in that country finds no buyers. People won't borrow to buy a car because borrowing has become costly. They also won't take their money out of the bank to buy one, because the high savings interest looks better. If spending doesn't fall, buyers for manufacturers wouldn't vanish; but they do, so the economy slows.
3. **Jobs fall.** Businesses don't borrow to expand and there are no buyers, so they **cut production**. With lower production they need less labor, so they start laying people off. New jobs aren't created and existing jobs decline.
4. **Inflation falls.**
   - Why: there are few buyers, and with no buyers, businesses are forced to sell the stock they hold rather than keep it. So the price rise (inflation) shrinks.
   - **What drives inflation up and down:** **demand and supply**. As demand for something rises, its price rises. In an economy, the more spenders there are, the more money people have and spend, and the more demand for goods grows.
   - **Low-rate example (cars):** if banks lend at low interest, everyone borrows and goes to buy cars. Car demand rises, there is competition among buyers, the price rises, and the manufacturer sells to whoever offers the better price.
   - **High-rate case:** banks aren't lending the way they were, people don't borrow at high rates, spenders fall, and competition falls. Businesses just want to sell what they have; they certainly won't raise prices. So inflation falls.
5. **Currency is supported.** When inflation falls, the country's currency gets support: prices have eased, so what we can buy with our money rises in value.

- **Net result of a rate hike:** the country's currency **rises** and inflation **falls**.
- **Negative side:** jobs fall and the economy slows. That is why the central bank doesn't simply raise rates: raising rates without thought puts a big strain on the economy (fewer jobs, a slower economy).

### 2.3 When the Central Bank Cuts Rates
- The **cost of borrowing falls**, and everyone borrows. Businesses begin borrowing if they find a bank offering low interest, and individuals borrow too.
- **Spending rises.** Instead of saving in a bank, it is better to buy goods, since saving gives little or no interest. Banks may even lend at low interest.
- With people spending, the **economy is stimulated**. People come and buy cars and houses, so the **real estate market** and the **car market** revive.
- **Many jobs** start being created. With many buyers, a car factory may even expand its factory and hire additional people to better supply the demand. Jobs rise and the economy grows.
- **But inflation begins.** Many people have money and many buy. With more buyers there is competition, and the manufacturer sells to whoever offers more. So the asset's price starts to rise.
  - Example: many people want a house and borrow from banks, and when they come to the real estate market, house prices **rise**. The same applies to cars.
- When inflation rises, the **currency weakens** in proportion.

### 2.4 Contractionary vs Expansionary Policy
- Both cases have been seen:
  - **Rate hike**: people stop spending, money collects in banks, inflation falls, and this **slows and contracts the economy**. This is called a **contractionary policy**.
  - **Rate cut**: more people borrow and spend, the economy becomes strongly stimulated, and more jobs are created. This is called an **expansionary policy**: the economy expands.

### 2.5 The Inflation "Sweet Spot"
- A central bank doesn't want inflation to be **zero**. Zero inflation means the economy is **dead** and very **stagnant**.
- It also doesn't want inflation to rise **beyond capacity**. If it does, everyone can't afford to buy, purchasing power is eroded, and people who can pay vanish. That can **put the brakes on** the economy too.
- So central banks want inflation in a **sweet spot** in the middle.
- Many central banks target inflation at this level. The **US Fed** and the **ECB (European Central Bank)** target **2%**. They don't want inflation at zero or rising.
- **US example:** after Covid, US inflation reached about **9%**. The central bank acted aggressively and brought it back to about 2%. After that, Trump's policy started pushing it up again, but the "sweet spot" is 2%.
- **Chart homework:** look at the US Fed's rate decisions (hikes and cuts) and the ECB's decisions on the charts, and see the change.
  - After Covid ended, as mentioned, inflation started rising strongly, reaching about 9% (US inflation).
  - The US central bank raised its interest rate from about **0.25%** and kept raising it aggressively, starting around **March 2022**. Go to the chart and see what implication it had on the currency.
  - The **ECB** followed the Fed and began raising its rates in **July 2022**. Go and see the implication on the **euro**.
  - Doing this will confirm that you understand the relationship between interest, the economy, interest, and currency value.

## 3. Real Interest Rate
- A simple metric: a country's **interest rate minus inflation**.
- **Ethiopia example:** most banks pay about **7%** a year on savings. But inflation was reported to have reached about **30%** (it is falling now). If I put **100,000 birr** in the bank, I get about **7,000** a year in interest. But that 100,000 birr also loses value: it depletes about 30%. An item bought for 100,000 birr today costs 30% more next year. So I haven't really earned interest: even with the interest added, the money can't buy what 100,000 birr bought then.
- **Real interest = current interest − inflation percentage.** If the result is **negative**, that country's currency is **not attractive**, and **investment there is not attractive**.
- **Bond example:** if I buy a government bond or T-bill paying about 10% interest, but that country's currency depreciates and loses 20% over the year, I have lost 10%.
- So we need to look at the **real interest rate**: the interest rate a country's currency gives us minus its inflation risk. If it is negative, investment attractiveness falls.

## 4. Carry Trade
- Another concept to understand. The carry trade idea matters especially in **USD/JPY**.
- **Idea:** borrow from a country with a **low interest rate** and invest in a country with a **high interest rate**.
- **USD/JPY example:** Japan's interest rate was very low, near **0.x%**. In the US, rates had risen to **4-point-something percent** or so (as mentioned earlier).
- An investor borrows in Japan (paying about 0% interest), converts the money to USD, and invests in the US, earning a much larger interest. This is very profitable: the interest paid is tiny, and the interest earned is large. That is why the USD/JPY pair was strongly appreciating (the USD) for about **3–4 years**, with a steep upward movement over the long term, because the **carry trade** was being run in it.
- This strategy is called the **carry trade**. It is not a real "strategy" for individuals. It is something **large institutions** with access to both countries do, since they invest in both. We retail traders can't go and borrow in Japan and invest in the US. We don't have that access.
- But it has a **big influence on the market**: institutions pull money out of Japan (selling JPY) and invest in the US, so USD demand rises while JPY is sold. The USD/JPY chart was **bullish** (the dollar appreciating, the yen depreciating).
- **Recent change:** around the time of recording, **Japan** has also started to raise its interest rate. As the carry trade balance shifts, USD/JPY starts to **fall back**, because positions were heavily built in the carry trade and are now unwinding.
